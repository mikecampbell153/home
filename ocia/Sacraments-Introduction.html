Tonight is an introduction to the Sacraments and what they have in common - a big picture view. In the Catechism, the introduction to the Sacraments is primarily about Liturgy. The relationship between Liturgy, Mass, Eucharist, and the other Sacraments is so integrated that it can at time feel like the terms are interchangeable. I think the Liturgy and the Sacraments are distinct but inseparable. The Church teaches:

The liturgy is the summit toward which the activity of the Church is directed… it is the font from which all her power flows … The Liturgy moves the faithful, filled with the paschal sacraments, to be one in holiness… From the liturgy, therefore, and especially from the Eucharist … grace is poured forth upon us… (SC.10).

Scripture Reading

Tonight I thought we would start with the story of Jesus' appearance on the Road to Emmaus from the Gospel of Luke. The Church's liturgical tradition has often read this story as a primordial liturgy of the Mass.

This story is found in the last chapter of Luke's Gospel and occurs on the day of Jesus' Resurrection from the dead - the first Easter Sunday. Early in the morning, Jesus appeared to the holy women at the tomb, then individually to Mary Magdalene, Saint Peter, and these two disciples.

These two disciples were leaving Jerusalem. Luke tells us they were looking sad and had lost hope that Jesus was the Messiah. Jesus walks with them but they are prevented from recognizing Him. While Jesus was walking with them, He opened the Scriptures to them. They invited Jesus to stay with them where Jesus breaks bread, their eyes were opened, and they recognized Him in the breaking of bread.

As we listen to the Gospel, listen for one word or phrase, especially with the perspective of the Liturgy. Listen for elements of the Mass. And if be open to share after the reading.

On this list, I highlighted the ones that are common to all sacraments. I think Confession is the shortest rite, but even it has:

  • a gathering — the priest and the penitent,
  • an introductory rite - greeting by the priest, sign of the cross, and then the preferred start: forgive me father for I have sinned; it has been a year since my last confession,

SLIDE - Mass, Revelation & Emmaus

Again, the two main elements of the Road to Emmaus are Liturgy of the Word and Liturgy of the Eucharist. The Mass is one liturgical celebration with with introduction, one conclusion, and two liturgies.

Please take this with a grain of salt, and a few might be a stretch; but I listed the elements that I found in the story of the Road to Emmaus. There is a gathering of two with Jesus, the high priest; on Sunday; there is a back-and-forth dialogue, Jesus opens the Scriptures to them.

Most importantly, Saint Luke wants us to recognize Jesus in the Eucharist, in the breaking of bread. In Luke 24, Saint Luke uses the Church’s familiar Eucharistic pattern: "he took bread, blessed and broke it, and gave it to them" (Lk 24:30). These are the same words Jesus used at the Last Supper (Lk 22:19), the same tradition Saint Paul handed on to the Corinthians (1 Cor 11:23-24), and the same actions the Church continues to celebrate in every Mass.

Lastly, let's see what the Catechism writes about the connection between Revelation and the Liturgy:

The book of Revelation of St. John, read in the Church's liturgy, first reveals to us, "A throne stood in heaven, with one seated on the throne: the Lord God." It then shows the Lamb, "standing, as though it had been slain": Christ crucified and risen, the one high priest of the true sanctuary, the same one "who offers and is offered, who gives and is given." Finally it presents "the river of the water of life . . . flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb," one of most beautiful symbols of the Holy Spirit (CCC.1137).

Two Disciples; Jesus' Presence

SLIDE - Two Disciples

Back to the story. There are two words that stood out to me that we will spend a few minutes reflecting on what can be gleaned from the Catechism. As an accountant by trade, the two words are actually numbers - the number two and the number six.

Saint Luke tells us that Jesus appeared to two disciples on the Road to Emmaus.

Why two? From a literal and practical point of view, it could be as simple as needing two witnesses to validate a story. Mosaic law requires two people to accuse somebody of wrongdoing. Jesus repeats this law in Matthew 18:20.

However, Jesus does not merely repeat the law; He fulfills and deepens it, giving it new life through His own presence. He says, "For where there are two or three gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them." In this story, we see that play out first by Jesus's presence in bodily form as He walks with the disciples. Then He vanishes from their sight and remains present in a new, real, and substantial way in the breaking of bread. The first post-resurrection Eucharist.

With that observation, let's see what the Catechism says.

SLIDE 1085

In paragraph 1085, the Catechism reads, "In the Liturgy Christ signifies and makes present His own Paschal Mystery." End quote. Liturgy signifies and makes present the Death and Resurrection of Jesus. The phrase signifies and makes present is liturgical and sacramental - perceptible signs, words and actions, that make present the grace they signify through the power and working of the Holy Spirit.

The Catechism goes on to teach that the Paschal Mystery is a unique event in history. Jesus is fully God and fully Man. His death and resurrection were historical events that occurred in history. Whereas all other historical events pass away, the Paschal Mystery transcends time and is made present in all of them. It abides, or lives forever, and it draws everything toward life. As Saint Augustine says, "Our hearts are restless until they find rest in God."

SLIDE 1088

In paragraph 1088, the Catechism goes on to teach how Jesus is present in the Liturgy.

Jesus is present in the minister. So much so that the priest speaks in the first person during the sacraments… I baptize you, I absolve you, this is my body. The Church term for this is "in persona Christi" - that is - in the Person of Christ. As such, it is Christ Who baptizes through His Church in the liturgy and sacrament. It is Jesus who forgives our sins in confession. It is Jesus who strengthens us for our pilgrim journey through Confirmation.

Next in the list, Jesus is especially present in the Eucharistic species – the bread and wine. At the moment of consecration, the bread and wine become for us the body, blood, soul, and divinity of Jesus in a true, real, and substantial way – the Church calls transubstantiation.

Next, Jesus is present in the Scripture readings. He is the Eternal Word of God. It is Jesus Himself Who speaks to us through His Word. The Catechism teaches,

Through all the words of Sacred Scripture, God speaks only one single Word. … For this reason, the Church has always venerated the Scriptures as she venerates the Lord's Body. She never ceases to present to the faithful the bread of life, taken from the one table of God's Word and Christ's Body. … Still, the Christian faith is not a "religion of the book." Christianity is the religion of the "Word" of God, a word which is "not a written and mute word, but the Word which is incarnate and living". (CCC.102,103,108)

As a personal anecdote, I remember to this day hearing how the Church gives us a visible sign of Jesus's presence in the Mass as the priest processes down the aisle with the Crucifix held high in front him and then the Book of the Gospels is processed before it's reading, we see Jesus's presence in the Crucifix, in the priest, and in the Scriptures.

SLIDE 1090

In the earthly liturgy we share in a foretaste of that heavenly liturgy which is celebrated in the Holy City of Jerusalem toward which we journey as pilgrims, where Christ is sitting at the right hand of God, Minister of the sanctuary and of the true tabernacle. (CCC.1090)

Paragraph 1090 is reminds us, that the earthly liturgy is pilgrim journey, to the Heavenly Liturgy which is celebrated in the Holy City of Jerusalem where Jesus is Minister of the Sanctuary and of the true tabernacle.

Finally, the Catechism quotes Matthew 18:20. Where two or three are gathered in my name, there I am with them. Jesus is present when the Church gathers to pray and worship. Christ, the head, is present as we gathered together, His body. The total Christ.

We have now come full circle returning to the the two disciples on the Road to Emmaus. Jesus became present to these two disciples as they traveled from Jerusalem and back to Jerusalem. In summary, the first key takeaway is that the Liturgy signifies and makes Jesus present.

SLIDE - Liturgical Poem

The Six Days of Creation

The next word that strikes me in this passage is the number six, which brings me to the second point of liturgy -- all of creation is ordered toward worship, toward liturgy. The six days of creation in the first chapter of Genesis, records that God created the heavens and the earth.

From the liturgical poem of the first creation to the canticles of the heavenly Jerusalem, the inspired authors proclaim the plan of salvation as one vast divine blessing. (CCC.1079)

From the liturgical poem of the first creation to the canticles of the heavenly Jerusalem

In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth. The Scripture says that the earth was without form and void.

The first three days of creation gives the formless earth its form. The second three days of creation fill the void. The pattern is poetic, liturgical, and sacramental. Each day has a rhythm:

  • And God said let there be…
  • And so it was…
  • God called or God blessed…
  • God saw that it was good…
  • There was evening and morning…
  • One day, second day, third day, and so on.

Going through each day:

The formation of light on the first day is filled with the Sun, Moon, and Stars on the fourth day.

The formation of the firmament on the second day is filled with the birds of the air and fish of the sea on the fifth day.

The formation of land and vegetation on the third day is filled with animals and man on the sixth day.

Not only does the Catechism call this the poem of the first creation, it says the liturgical poem of the first creation. The first creation is the formation of a cosmic (cf.CCC.1189) sanctuary. A sanctuary that is repeated in the construction of the tabernacle under Moses and the Temple under King Solomon.

Christ is the true temple of God, "the place where his glory dwells"; by the grace of God, Christians also become the temples of the Holy Spirit, living stones out of which the Church is built. (CCC.1197)

To repeat, Christ is the true temple of God. We Christians also become temple of the Holy Spirit.

The creation of light on the first day is the opening of the Sanctuary. The firmament on the second day is the vault of the sanctuary, the choir loft. The land and vegetation of the third day is the table of plenty; the first fruits of creation. On the fourth day, the sun, moon, and stars are the liturgical ministers setting the days and seasons for the feasts and festivals. On the fifth day, the vault is filled with voices of praise. On the sixth day, the sanctuary is filled with participants and with the priestly rulers of creation. "Male and female He created them, be fruitful and multiply, fill the earth and subdue it, and have dominion over it."

God then placed man in the Garden of Eden. The Garden is the Holy of Holies. This liturgical poem ends with God's presence in the Garden of Eden. This presence is lost in the fall. Adam and Eve are banned from the Garden. The rest of the story is the a story of salvation.

Noah builds an ark patterned after the first creation with three levels, and He fills it with animals. Moses builds a Tabernacle and God is present in the Ark of the Covenant. Solomon builds a Temple, a house of cedar saying, "But will God indeed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain thee; how much less this house which I have built!" (1.Kings.8.27). Jesus fulfills these words - "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us." (Jn.TBD) Literal meaning is Jesus 'tabernacled' among us. "Him whom the heavens cannot contain, the womb of one woman bore" (Saint Augustine, Sermon 184).

SLIDE - Work of Creation

All of creation is ordered to Worship. The Catechism goes on to teach that,

Creation was fashioned with a view to the sabbath and therefore for the worship and adoration of God. Worship is inscribed in the order of creation. As the rule of St. Benedict says, nothing should take precedence over "the work of God", that is, solemn worship. This indicates the right order of human concerns.
There is another day – the eighth day. But for us a new day has dawned: the day of Christ's Resurrection. The seventh day completes the first creation. The eighth day begins a new creation. The work of creation culminates in the greater work of redemption. (cf. CCC.345-349)

Jesus said, "He who believes in me will also do the works that I do; and greater works than these will he do, because I go to the Father. Whatever you ask in my name, I will do it, that the Father may be glorified in the Son. (John.14.12-13)

Early fathers believed regeneration of fallen man surpasses physical healings in eternal significance. Augustine notes, “To bring the sinner from death to life is a greater work than to raise a dead body” (Tract. in Joann. 72.2; Biblehub.com).

But before we leave Liturgy, let's define Liturgy and review what the Catechism teaches.

ASK FOR DEFINITION OF LITURGY

SLIDE - What is Liturgy (2)

Liturgy is the official public worship of the Church, the participation of People of God in the Work of God.

As I was preparing for this talk, it was a struggle to find a concise stable definition of liturgy. The universal catechism states, "participation of the people of God in the work of God". However, as I asked around, the phrase "official public worship of the Church" was more common. Instead of choosing between the two, I took the liberty of combining.

SLIDE - What is Liturgy (Next)

Official Public Worship

To quote Pope Pius XII in his encyclical Mediator Dei,

The sacred liturgy is the public worship which our Redeemer as Head of the Church renders to the Father, as well as the worship which the community of the faithful renders to its Founder, and through Him to the heavenly Father.

In the Sacred liturgy Jesus Christ as Head of the Church worships the Father and the community worships the Father through Jesus, the Founder of the Church. I had to sit with this quote to even start to understand how this can be.

Jesus, being fully God and fully human, is the Perfect Person offering the perfect sacrifice to God His Father. This is ultimately a mystery hidden in the hypostatic union - One Person; two natures. We, the People of God, as a community, worship our Founder, Jesus Christ, through Him, to the heavenly Father. As we hear at every Mass, "Through Him, and with Him, and in Him, O God, almighty Father, in the unity of the Holy Spirit."

The Catechism teaches that Liturgy is an action of the whole Christ. In Latin: Christus totus (CCC.1136) The whole Christ and His mystical body – the Church – the head and the members. We are all one body. This includes those who are already in heaven celebrating without signs where liturgy is wholly communion and feast. (cf.CCC.1136)

The meaning of the word public in this context is that it is governed by the Church in her liturgical books. Like public works performed by the state on behalf of its people. As opposed to out in the open verses in private. For example, the Liturgy of the Hours can be done in private even though it is liturgy, public worship.

SLIDE - Participation

Which leads us to the second phrase of the definition of liturgy – a participation of the People of God. By our baptism, we are all called to offer a sacrifice of praise and worship to God. Worship is both a right and an obligation. Worship is giving to God what is due to God. All of creation is ordered to worship and all of creation is to be returned to God. At every Mass, we hear, "it is right and just, our duty and our salvation to give You praise."

Finally, in the spirit of participation, the Church asks of us "to come the liturgy consciously prepared to make their thoughts agree with what we see and hear, and to cooperate with divine grace." It's the old saying, you get out of it what you put into it.

I think the emphasis here is on cooperating with divine grace. While preparation is key, and we should do our part, our efforts are a response to grace.

SLIDE - Work of God

Lastly, liturgy is the Work of God. Liturgy is rightly seen as an exercise of Jesus Christ, the High Priest.

God the Father is both the source of all of the blessings of creation and salvation, and He is the goal of liturgy (cf.CCC.1082). The Father continues His work of Creation in each and every one of us. God does not abandon his creatures to themselves. … At every moment, He upholds and sustains us, and enables us to act justly. He is faithful and just and will complete the work He began in us (cf.CCC.301, cf.Phil.1.6)).

Through the Holy Spirit, the People of God are sanctified through visible signs perceptible by the senses. Our bodies are temples of the Holy Spirit. He is at work within us. He is interceding for us for we do not know how to pray as we ought. (cf.Rom.8.26)

Lastly, we have already covered Jesus's work of redemption, the culmination of the first creation.

It may help to have a list of what is liturgy and what is not liturgy.

SLIDE - Liturgy

Liturgy is the Mass, Liturgy of the Hours, Official Blessings, Exorcisms, consecrations, and Liturgical Year.

Liturgy is not catechesis, OCIA, Bible studies, retreats, pilgrimages, and personal prayer, and private devotions.

These are all good and build community but they are not liturgy because they can be changed by the leaders, they do not need a priest, and do not follow official liturgical books.

SLIDE - Liturgy of the Hours

Really quickly, I wanted to give a brief introduction to the Liturgy of the Hours. We have mentioned it a couple times during our OCIA sessions, and I thought a brief overview would be helpful.

Closely tied to the Eucharist in the daily liturgical life of the Church is the Liturgy of the Hours, especially Morning and Evening Prayer. The Liturgy of the Hours ... prolongs the Eucharistic celebration, and leads us back to it.

The laity are invited to pray the Liturgy of the Hours, but it is not an obligation like parish priests. It takes about an hour each day in total spread over 5 prayers. That can make it challenging for the laity to pray. If it is something you are interested in starting, most online guides recommend starting with one prayer. The night prayer is the shortest and includes an examination of conscience. The morning prayer and evening prayers are the principal prayers. Finally, the online guides strongly encourage following what the Church has written and following an app or a book, instead of personalizing or changing the prayers. This is sound advice given that it is liturgy, or official prayer of the Church.

How We Worship?

SLIDE - How We Worship

The Church celebrates the liturgy using an abundance of signs, symbols, and rituals that speak of the mystery of God and divine transcendence on the one hand, and the unity of God with the worshiping community on the other. Since the Son of God honored us by becoming incarnate—the true visible image of the invisible God—we use these signs and symbols to help us experience God’s invisible presence.

For example, the signs that accompany the Mass Readings are the use of a beautiful book, a procession with the Book of the Gospels candles. The combination of word and action helps make visible the invisible action of Christ and the Holy Spirit. (USCCA.14)

To wrap up the liturgy, Catechism paragraph 1076 teaches: Christ manifests, makes present, and communicates his work of salvation through the liturgy of his Church. ... He acts through the sacraments". The Liturgy and the Sacraments are distinct but inseparable.

AUDIENCE PARTICIPATION

SLIDE - What is a Sacrament? (1A)

Ask the audience for a definition of a Sacrament. Repeat the definition:

A sacrament is an outward sign instituted by Christ to give grace.

Those of us who grew up with the Baltimore Catechism, or entered the Church prior to the promulgation of the Universal Catechism, have definition memorized. Other defintions are:

  • A sacrament is a visible sign of an invisible grace.
  • A sacrament is a sign of a sacred things.
  • The sacraments are efficacious signs of grace, instituted by Christ and entrusted to the Church, by which divine life is dispensed to us.

The purpose of the sacraments is to sanctify men, to build up the body of Christ, and to give worship to God; because they are signs they also instruct (SC.59).

Sacraments are the saving words and deeds of Jesus Christ. God accomplishes His greatest works in the simplest of words…

  • Let there be light,
  • Lazarus come out,
  • This is my body.

Sacraments have a visible and invisible reality. The visible part is what we can sense – see, touch, taste, and smell. For example, a parent loves a child – an invisible reality – but the hug of a parent is the visible reality of that love. The combination of word and action make visible the invisible action (USCCA.14).

The visible reality of the Sacraments are the matter and form, as well as the minister. They open our heart to the invisible reality, which is the grace that comes with the Sacraments.

What is Grace? Grace is God's initiative. Archbishop Fulton Sheen writes, "…grace [is] a supernatural gift bestowed on us through the merits of Jesus Christ for our salvation. … This life of Christ in the baptized soul." Grace is the free and loving gift He offers people to share in His Divine Life.

Faith is a gift of God, a supernatural virtue infused by him. Before this faith can be exercised, man must have the grace of God to move and assist him; he must have the interior helps of the Holy Spirit, who moves the heart and converts it to God, who opens the eyes of the mind … The preparation of man for the reception of grace is already a work of grace. (CCC.153,2001)

SLIDE - What is a Sacrament (2)

Efficacious Signs of Grace:

  • The confer what they signify. The signs are symbols, but not just symbols, the actually effect that which the symbolize. Water cleanses our bodies. Baptism cleanses our souls from sin.
  • The are efficacious because it is Jesus himself who is at work in the sacraments through the power of the Holy Spirit.
  • The term the Church uses for this action is Sacramental Grace.

Instituted by Christ and Entrusted to the Church:

  • The whole liturgical life of the Church revolves around the Eucharistic sacrifice and the sacraments. (CCC.1113)
  • The law of prayer is the law of faith: the Church believes as she prays. Liturgy is not just an expression of Tradition; it is one of the elements that form Tradition. (cf.CCC.1124)
  • Saint Paul writes to the Ephesians, "And he put all things beneath his feet and gave him as head over all things to the church" (Eph.1.23).

Divine Life is Dispensed to Us:

  • The mission of the Holy Spirit in the Liturgy is to prepare the assembly to encounter Christ. (CCC.1112)
  • The Word became flesh to make us "partakers of the divine nature" (CCC.460; cf. 2.Peter.1:4)
  • "For the Son of God became man so that we might become God.“(CCC.460; cf.St.Athanasius)

SLIDE - Seven Sacraments

There are seven sacraments.

The Sacraments are grouped into three groups: Initiation, Healing, and Service.

The Sacraments of Initiation are Baptism, Confirmation, and Eucharist. Or the big three. We have ___ being baptized! We have __ receiving confirmation. Truly exciting and a reminder of how beloved the catechumens are. They bring new life, renewed energy, and a fresh perspective to the parish.

The sacraments of healing are Penance, also called Confession and Reconciliation. And Anointing of the Sick.

The sacraments of service are marriage and holy orders.

The sacraments have four elements in common - form and matter; the minister; and the grace bestowed.

The matter of baptism is water. The form is the words, "I baptize you in the name of the Father Son and Holy Spirit. The ordinary minister is the priest. The extraordinary minister is anyone, if there is an emergency. The principal grace received is forgiveness of sins - mortal and venial sins – and new birth, or being born again, (cf.CCC.1262), and the gifts of the holy spirit. This sacrament leaves an indelible mark on the soul and is received only once.

Confirmation. The matter of confirmation is oil. The form is the words, "Be sealed with the gift of the Holy spirit." The ordinary minister is the Bishop. The extraordinary minister is the priest. The grace received is an increase and deepening of baptismal grace – the gifts of the Holy Spirit. The grace is also being rooted more firmly as children of God – crying Abba Father – and united more firmly to Christ (cf.CCC.1302-1304). Like baptism, it leaves an indelible mark on the soul, and it can only be received once.

The matter of the Eucharist is bread and wine. The form is the words "This is my body and this is the cup of my blood …" The bread and wine are consecrated separately, symbolizing the death of Jesus where He gave every drop of blood on the Cross. One of my favorite paragraphs from the Catechism reads:

The Church is born primarily of Christ's total self-giving for our salvation, anticipated in the institution of the Eucharist and fulfilled on the cross. "The origin and growth of the Church are symbolized by the blood and water which flowed from the open side of the crucified Jesus." "For it was from the side of Christ as he slept the sleep of death upon the cross that there came forth the 'wondrous sacrament of the whole Church.'" As Eve was formed from the sleeping Adam's side, so the Church was born from the pierced heart of Christ hanging dead on the cross (CCC.766).

The grace received are …

  • increases in union with the Lord,
    (no closer union with Jesus than to receive the Hidden Jesus under the roofs of our mouth at Holy Communion)
  • forgiveness of venial sins, and
    (one should only receive Eucharist when they are free from mortal sin, which is forgiven in baptism - that case for our Catechumens at Easter Vigil - or penance, for the rest of us)
  • protection from mortal sins.
  • it also reinforces the unity of the Church as the Mystical Body of Christ (CCC.1416).

The matter of Confession is contrition and penance. The form is the words, "I absolve you". The minister is the priest. The sacramental grace of Penance are:

  • reconciliation with God by which the penitent recovers grace;
  • reconciliation with the Church;
  • remission of the eternal punishment incurred by mortal sins;
  • remission, at least in part, of temporal punishments resulting from sin;
  • peace and serenity of conscience, and spiritual consolation;
  • an increase of spiritual strength for the Christian battle (CCC.1496).

The matter of the anointing of sick is oil. The form is the liturgical prayer of the minister asking for the special grace of this sacrament (CCC.1531). The minister is the priest. The special grace of the sacrament of the Anointing of the Sick are:

  • the uniting of the sick person to the passion of Christ, for his own good and that of the whole Church;
  • the strengthening, peace, and courage to endure in a Christian manner the sufferings of illness or old age;
  • the forgiveness of sins,
  • the restoration of health, if it is conducive to the salvation of his soul;
  • the preparation for passing over to eternal life (CCC. 1532).

The matter of Holy Orders the laying on of hands. As we learned two weeks ago, Bishops are the successors to the apostles and the laying on of hands is a visible sign of apostolic succession. The form is the prayer of consecration for the specific order. The principal grace is strength to carry out the duties of the office – to guide and defend the Church. Like baptism and confirmation, this sacrament leaves an indelible mark on the soul and can be received only once.

The matter of marriage consent of the spouses by which the partners mutually give themselves to each other (CCC.1627). The form is the vows and the "I do". The ministers are the bride and groom. The priest is the ordinary witness. The sacrament of Matrimony signifies the union of Christ and the Church. It gives spouses the grace to love each other with the love with which Christ has loved his Church; the grace of the sacrament thus perfects the human love of the spouses, strengthens their indissoluble unity, and sanctifies them on the way to eternal life (CCC.1661).
