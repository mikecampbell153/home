CFO Memoir: Of Mice & Men Finance Edition

The failure was textbook: a refusal to evolve from a hustle into a business. It began with a young man between jobs, a young family, and a single truck. A colleague took a chance on him, and one truck became two, then three, then twenty. The revenue grew enough to support a family of five and a few extended relatives. But the enterprise never matured. Despite hundreds of ancillary SKUs, it effectively sold one thing. There was no strategy, no succession plan, no governance, and no curiosity about what a real business required.

Then came tariffs, weather shocks, war‑driven supply volatility, and a long consolidation cycle. Without governance, without the ability to see the macro picture, and without the discipline to pivot, the company crossed the line where it became worth more dead than alive. A viable product that never became a viable business.

Over the past decade, dozens of founders in the sector sold to larger operators. One held out. Armed with borrowed mantras — it’s a volume business, gotta have it — he clung to the company like a small, fragile animal he believed he was protecting. He stroked it, petted it, and never recognized the strain he was placing on it. When personal crisis hit, he panicked. He squeezed too hard. And he crushed the very thing he believed he was saving.

It was Of Mice and Men in corporate form: a story where disordered attachment, not financial failure, ultimately kills the enterprise. The ripple effects will last for years. And the family will never be the same.

The unraveling began on an ordinary summer afternoon, when the founder’s golf cart was struck by a truck with enough force to make the accident nearly fatal. In a family system already stretched thin, the event acted like a fault‑line tremor: subtle at first, but destabilizing everything that depended on his emotional presence. The company entered its busiest season just as its leader was physically shaken and psychologically frayed.

The founder mirrors Lennie in the John Steinbeck book "Of Mice And Men". He was a hard worker and outwardly very strong. But inwardly, he was unable to function independently. He was impulsive, insecure, and fearful. When under duress, he would cling tighter to the things that soothed his fragile ego. His rabbits were vintage cars, his lake house, and his Florida condo - symbols of comfort, fantasy, and escape. His inability to regulate his emotions would result in lashing out at employees and family, using anger and chaos to regain control.

The week before June 9th carried its own omen. The owner of a local construction company, one of the founder's top five customers and the man who helped build the company in its early years—died after a short battle with cancer, less than a year from diagnosis. His funeral was scheduled for the next day.

The founder and his wife were at a resort in North Carolina with another top‑five customer and his wife. They were using a golf cart for transportation, not golf, when a truck t‑boned them. The passenger side, where the wives were seated, absorbed the full impact. Both women suffered extensive injuries and broken bones. The driver side was thrown clear, sustaining only minor injuries. His wife was airlifted to the nearest trauma center. He missed the funeral of his close friend and colleague.

To understand the impact of this accident, one must understand that the founder's wife was George in Steinbeck's story. She was the stabilizer, the protector, and the only structure the business ever had. She built and ran the back office, until she retired. She did not have the unhealthy attachment her husband. When she stepped away, the founder began to swing harder for the fence taking on more and more debt for a company that was being squeezed out of the market by an unstoppable multi-billion dollar duopoly. The accident was the moment that George and the workers left to go to the store leaving Lennie alone and unprotected from himself.

In the span of forty‑eight hours, the mortality of two top customers, two close friends, his wife, and himself converged. In a world where work, family, and friendship are fused into a single identity, the entire structure of who he was came under threat.

The days after the accident were disorienting. His wife could not be transported back to Louisville for surgery; the head trauma required intervention there. They were stuck. But the prognosis improved, and they returned home the following week.

A day or two later, the founder was in my office. It was business as usual—still unwilling to let go of the big jobs, still unwilling, or unable, to have a conversation about margin. As he stood to leave, he mentioned the accident for the first time. His only comment was how disappointed he was to have missed the funeral. It felt as if the threat to his company was more traumatizing than the injuries to his wife.

That is when the slow burn of industry consolidation, Covid upheaval, and macro geopolitics accelerated into a rapid nose dive. The founder simultaneously asked the bank to nearly double his debt position with an increase on the line of credit to fund the big projects, the down payment on a new building, and the building itself. For the first time in their relationship, the bank said "no". They froze the line and began implementing structure; starting with vendor payment terms and inventory levels. These were two things the bank had repeatedly asked for.

The bank's lender was Steinbeck's landowner. He was the one with the resources to fund the dream. Unlike the sotry, thought, the lender allowed himself to get dragged into the founder's fantasy with frequent weekend outings on the boat. The professional lines were blurred and the lender became like a financial advisor to the founder. The company was Curley's wife - aspirational, idealized, fragile, and crushed in a moment of panic.

The debt increase would have crushed the company under its weight, having already breached its DSCR covenant. The ask communicated to the bank that the founder was no longer fit to run the company. They don't care about the accident, they only care about the numbers. They then did the unthinkable. They bypassed the founder and scheduled a meeting with the CFO and a credit officer. To the founder who has no boundary between personal and business, this was an unforgivable treacherous act of disloyalty. That was the beginning of the end for the CFO.

The CFO had been watching gross margin closely since first quarter of 2025. Sales had rebounded from the rough first quarter of 2024, but margin collapsed year over year. He started asking questions - another sign of disloyalty - trying to more fully understand the industry, the producers, the contractors. One question needed an answer: Was the decline cyclical or structural. Early signs were pointing to structural and the CFO dusted off his planned exit, something he had delayed after a successful company sale in his previous role. Before June 9th, the runway was at least a year. After June 9th, the runway went quickly from months, to weeks, to days.

The credit officer met with the CFO. They were like-minded and understood the numbers - something the family never did. Five years of declining EBITDA, 10-day DPO, and big projects with low margin. One the one hand, the credit officer was reassuring, let's not hit the panic button yet. On the other hand, he used terms like mismanaged and implementing structure. The CFO could see the writing on the wall, this was the first step toward liquidation given the enterprise value had dropped below the value of net assets.

The CFO was like Slim - stable, competent, and unaffected by the dysfunction around him. He was not drawn into the drama and kept an arms-length. The credit officer was like the lynch mob - the inevitable institutional force that arrives when harm can no longer be contained. The future liquidation would be the shot in the clearing. It would be the end of a company, and for a man who's entire identity is fused with the business, it would be the end of who he was as a person. Not a morbid end but something that will take years to redefine.

The straw that broke the camel's back was the founder's son. He was a hot head, destabilizing, and disliked by all. He had the title of Sales VP even though he had only one customer. It was the customer who was in the accident with the founder. This one detail communicates the whole dynamic of the company. The founder would never relinquish control. He gave the kids titles, salaries, and phantom stock, but he never gave them authority, training, nor any real attempt at succession. After the CFO aligned with the bank and started forcing tighter cash management, his son "went into a tizzy" to quote his older sister, the President.

The President was technically the CFO's boss. But with little aptitude, no executive function, and no leadership, she was an irrelevant figurehead. Hence the reason she does not even enter the story until here. She marched into the CFO's office to blame him for her brother's unraveling. That was when the CFO's runway went from weeks to days. The little brother was so volatile that he literally ran into a wall trying to avoid the CFO. The system was destabilized to the point of becoming unsafe, at least emotionally.

The founder's son was Curley. He was the insecure heir, destabilizing aggression, conflict escalation, and no self-awareness. The two older daughters - President and VP - were the workers in Steinbeck's tale - tactical, powerless, and along for the ride. The workers were merely irrelevant backdrops to a tragic story.

Then came the warning shot. It was not the shot in the clearing but it was a devastating blow. The bank met with the founder and went over the numbers. The fact that the bank was as blunt as they were communicates that they had moved from supporting growth to mitigating risk. The message they delivered was a near-fatal blow to a man who could not separate himself from his company. They told him (1) your daughter cannot run the company, let alone finance, (2) the company is worthless, and (3) they threw out a ball park post-covid fantasy valuation. It wasn’t financial guidance; it was an existential blow. The founder couldn’t metabolize succession, valuation, or family critique without volatility, and the bankers didn’t read the room.

Two days later, needing a scapegoat, the founder gave the CFO an ultimatum - either be ambushed in a family meeting to "clear the air" or "we don't want you here." The Monday morning meetings were performative rituals where the founder imposed his will. That afternoon, the CFO executed his contingency plan. He tendered his resignation, not with the founder, but with his daughter. On his resignation day, the lender’s boss said this was the worst thing that could have happened to the company at that moment. He asked if there was any attempt to retain the CFO. He recognized the dysfunctional avoidant behavior of the founder to the CFO. The lender and his boss, as they debriefed, could only shake their heads and say WTF, the founder has lost his way. Finally, he admitted what we already knew: they had waited too long to say no. By then, the unraveling was irreversible.
