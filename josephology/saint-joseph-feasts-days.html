Appendix: The Liturgical Feasts of Saint Joseph

1. The Solemnity of Saint Joseph, Husband of the Blessed Virgin Mary — March 19

The Solemnity of Saint Joseph on March 19 is the oldest and primary feast dedicated to Joseph. It appears in Western liturgical calendars by the 10th century and becomes universal by the 13th century. The feast honors Joseph’s vocation as spouse of Mary and guardian of the Incarnate Word. In 1870, Blessed Pius IX declared Saint Joseph Patron of the Universal Church through the decree Quemadmodum Deus. This act did not create a new feast day; rather, it attached Joseph’s universal patronage to the already-existing March 19 solemnity. Its placement near the Feast of the Annunciation (March 25) is a natural theological pairing with Marian feasts.

2. Feast of Saint Joseph, Patron of the Universal Church (Third Sunday After Easter) — Suppressed 1955

Following Pius IX’s 1870 declaration, a distinct feast of Saint Joseph under the title Patron of the Universal Church was added to the Roman calendar and celebrated on the third Sunday after Easter. This feast emphasized Joseph’s protective role over the Mystical Body of Christ. In 1955, Pope Pius XII suppressed this separate feast. Its theological content was absorbed into the March 19 solemnity, which already carried Joseph’s patronage. Interestingly, the Gospel Reading for Third Sunday after Easter is typically Luke's account of the Road to Emmaus featuring Joseph's brother, Clopas.

3. Feast of the Patronage of Saint Joseph (Regional, 1680–1955)

A separate feast known as the Patronage of Saint Joseph developed regionally beginning in 1680, particularly within Spain and territories influenced by Spanish devotional life. This feast highlighted Joseph’s guardianship over Christian families and communities. Like the universal patronage feast, the regional Patronage of Saint Joseph was suppressed in 1955 by Pius XII, consolidating Josephine celebrations into the March 19 solemnity.

4. Feast of Saint Joseph the Worker — May 1 (Instituted 1955)

On May 1, 1955, Pope Pius XII instituted the Feast of Saint Joseph the Worker. Announced in an address to the Association of Italian Catholic Workers, the feast was intended to Christianize May Day, which had become associated with communist labor ideology. The feast emphasizes the dignity of human labor, Joseph as a model of workers, and Catholic social teaching. This feast is now universal and stands alongside March 19 as one of the two principal Josephine celebrations in the Roman Rite.

5. Feast of the Chaste Heart of Saint Joseph (Private Devotion; Josephite Orders)

The Feast of the Chaste Heart of Saint Joseph has never been part of the universal Roman calendar, nor has it been formally suppressed. It remains a legitimate private devotion and is celebrated by Josephite religious communities such as the Oblates of Saint Joseph. Its liturgical placement is in rhythm with the three hearts of the Holy Family:

  • Sacred Heart of Jesus — Friday after Corpus Christi
  • Immaculate Heart of Mary — Saturday after the Sacred Heart
  • Chaste Heart of Joseph — Wednesday after the Sacred Heart

This devotional sequence highlights the spiritual unity of Jesus, Mary, and Joseph. It also honors the ancient daily devotions for Fridays, Saturdays, and Wednesdays.

6. Commemoration of the Dormition of Saint Joseph — June 19/20 (Eastern Traditions)

The Dormition (Falling Asleep) of Saint Joseph is an ancient tradition preserved in several Eastern Christian communities. Its origins trace to 4th–6th century devotional and apocryphal literature, most notably the History of Joseph the Carpenter, an early Egyptian text that narrates Joseph’s peaceful death in the presence of Jesus and Mary. It is not a part of the Roman Rite, but historically significant for Josephology. It provides one of the earliest narrative traditions about Joseph’s final days.
