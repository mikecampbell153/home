Summa Josephologica

Prologue: Why the World Needs a Theology of Joseph

Josephology - the systematic study of Joseph, husband of Mary and foster father of Jesus Christ - is a surprisingly new branch of theology. It formally began in 1870 when Pope Pius IX issued Quemadmodum Deus, the decree declaring Joseph Patron of the Universal Church. The purpose of that document was simple but profound: to anchor the Church’s life, protection, and mission in the quiet strength of the man who guarded the Incarnate Word. That moment opened the door for a new theological discipline—one still in its infancy, still developing its methods, and still ripe for exploration.

In this work, I propose an outline for a systematic study of Josephology. It is admittedly a bold undertaking, yet the absence of such a framework—either online or in print—reveals a genuine theological gap. My approach begins with Scripture, examining Joseph’s clan at the Cross and the related geography of his kinship network. I then walk through five pivotal moments from his Angelic Annunciation to the Finding the Boy Jesus in the temple. These moments follow the same rhythm as the Joyful Mysteries of the Rosary, allowing Joseph’s role in salvation history to be contemplated in union with Marian revelation.

These five moments opens the door to Old Testament typologies that illuminate his mission and identity. With this foundation in place, we can articulate five Josephine Pious Beliefs, not as dogmas, but as subordinate yet meaningful parallels to Marian dogmatic definitions. In other words, rather than reinventing the wheel, we can apply the proven systematic structure of Mariology to the emerging field of Josephology, giving it coherence, clarity, and theological rigor.

Finally, I explore how Joseph personifies the Josephine writings of Popes Leo XIII and John Paul II on Christian Anthropology both released on Feast of the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, August 15, 1889 and 1989, respectively.

These contexts open up Joseph’s role not only as father and protector, but as a man whose daily work formed the human environment of the Incarnate Son. Since this project is focused on developing the framework for systematic study, it is purposely light on academic references. My hope is that this framework will assist in systematizing and organizing existing theological studies and devotional reflections. I humbly approach this in imitation of Saint Joseph who worked silently in the shadows of Jesus and Mary building a solid foundation on which the root of Jesse could grow in wisdom and statuure among men and God and a foundation on which to build the Kingdom of God.

Josephology Protects Christology and Mariology

Joseph is a silent figure in Scripture and in most of Christian history. Joseph's silence speaks volumes about his protective role with the mystery of the Incarnation, the Mother of God, and their Divine Son - as head of the Holy Family.

After the hidden years in Nazareth and he remained silent until 1870. It took this long to solidify the Marian dogmas of Perpetual Virginity and Divine Motherhood. Joseph needed to remain hidden to protect these dogmas which are ultimately Christolgical - Jesus is fully God and fully man; One Divine Person, two natures; God from God, Light from Light, True God from True God. Begotten not made.

Then in 1870, the Church brought Joseph out of the hidden shadows to solidify the Immaculate Conception and Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary. The proximity of the declaration of the Immaculate Conception in 1854 and the declaration of Joseph as Patron of the Universal Church was truly correlative. The Church says as much by choosing the December 8, the Feast Day of the Immaculate Conception as the date for the declaration of Joseph as Patron of the Universal Church.

Again, in 1955, just months after Pope Pius XII's declaration of the Assumption of the Blessed Virgin Mary, the pope instituted the feast of Saint Joseph, the worker. Both declarations were to teach the world about human dignity and Christian anthropology. The bodily assumption of Mary (who is not unique in this regard, unlike her uniqueness as Immaculate Conception) teaches the People of God that man is a composite of body and soul, created for God and by God to know God, love God, and serve God in the life and the next. Likewise, the Saint Joseph the Worker, is direct opposition to communism and secular humanism that denies the spiritual and the supernatural.

Finally, the declaration of Saint Joseph as Patron of the Universal Church protects the doctrine of Mary Mediatrix of all Grace. This doctrine is the ordinary universal teaching of the Magesterium, only lacking a solemn definition to be elevated to dogma. Her title Most Gracious Advocate - that we recite with every Rosary and in the Liturgy of the Hours night prayer (Compline) nearly all year - is the same substance as the title Mediatrix. While Mary's role as Mediatrix is unique because of her uniqueness as Mother of God and Immaculate Conception, it is complemented by Joseph's role as Patron of the Universal Church.

This motherhood of Mary in the order of grace continues uninterruptedly from the consent which she loyally gave at the Annunciation and which she sustained without wavering beneath the cross, until the eternal fulfillment of all the elect. Taken up to heaven she did not lay aside this saving office but by her manifold intercession continues to bring us the gifts of eternal salvation ... Therefore the Blessed Virgin is invoked in the Church under the titles of Advocate, Helper, Benefactress, and Mediatrix. (CCC 969, LG 62)
Mary's function as mother of men in no way obscures or diminishes this unique mediation of Christ, but rather shows its power. But the Blessed Virgin's salutary influence on men ... flows forth from the superabundance of the merits of Christ, rests on his mediation, depends entirely on it, and draws all its power from it. (CCC 970, LG 60)
it is right to say, that nothing at all of that very great treasury of all grace which the Lord brought us--for 'grace and truth came through Jesus Christ' [Jn 1.17]--nothing is imparted to us except through Mary, since God so wills, so that just as no one can come to the Father except through the Son, so in general, no one can come to Christ except through His Mother. (Pope Leo XIII)

Joseph's Clan Network - Holy Women at the Cross

Throughout the Gospels, we are gradually introduced to the network of kinspersons foundational to Jesus building His Kingdom. At the Cross, four major characters converge. As we look into each of the Holy Women, we learn that each is related to Joseph and more than half the apostles are called from this clan. Mary Magdalene stands alone but gives us another perspective on Joseph.

These four women also represent the five major geographies that become a most important back drop of Salvation History - Bethlehem, Nazareth, and Capernaum in Galilee, and Jerusalem and Bethany in Judea. We will also look at Sepphoris and Samaria. These geographies provide key datapoints for understanding the formation of the Messiah under Joseph's fatherhood, occupation, and vocation.

Joseph's Joyful Mysteries

Joseph is present with Mary at three of the joyful mysteries of the Rosary. We will reflect on these mysteries from Joseph's perspective. Joseph alone receives his own Angelic Annunciation that is both a beautiful contrast and complementary of the Angel Gabriel's Annunciation to Mary. Joseph's presence in the Visitation is disputed so I, therefore, replace that moment with Joseph's dreams in which an angel instructed him to flee to Egypt and to Return from Egypt. These two moments show us his protection of the Holy Family and docility to the Will of God.

Joseph in the Old Testament Pattern

Josephology also uncovers deep biblical parallels. In this work, I will explore how Joseph stands in continuity with:

  • Adam - the first steward of creation
  • Noah - the righteous man who shelters life
  • Joseph - the guardian who preserves God’s people
  • Jesse - the father whose lineage leads to the Messiah

These typologies reveal Joseph not as a footnote but as a hinge where Old Testament patterns converge and the New Covenant begins.

John Paul II repeatedly returned to Gaudium et Spes 22, and Josephology takes up the same thread:

Christ, the final Adam, … fully reveals man to man himself and makes his supreme calling clear.

Joseph: The Hinge of Human History

Josephology ultimately asks a set of anthropological questions: what it means to be human, what it means to be redeemed, and who Jesus is—fully God and fully man—and what Joseph’s fatherhood reveals about that mystery.

In the Gospel of Luke, Jesus teaches:

“Whoever seeks to save his life will lose it, and whoever loses his life will preserve it.” (Luke 17:33, RSV)

Here the Catholic Church gives us a crucial interpretive key in Gaudium et Spes §24:

“Man…cannot fully find himself except through a sincere gift of himself.”

Joseph is the first man of the New Covenant to live that anthropology in full. His entire vocation—work, fatherhood, obedience, protection—is structured self-gift. He does not “seek to save his life”; he gives it away in service to the Incarnate Word and His Mother. And in that gift, Joseph becomes the model of redeemed humanity.

Josephology, then, is not merely devotional. It is a return to the text—a way of seeing how the quiet self-gift of Joseph reveals the shape of Christian life and the shape of Christian anthropology.

Summing It Up

Josephology is not niche. It is not sentimental. It is not devotional fluff.

It is a framework for Christian anthropology, a lens for understanding the Incarnation, and a way of recovering the dignity of ordinary life—work, family, responsibility, and quiet faithfulness.

This work will explore Joseph not as a statue in a corner, but as a theological key; one capable of unlocking a deeper understanding of Christ and a deeper understanding of ourselves. It is my humble attempt at a Summa Josephologica. It is not a scholarly work but an organized framework for studying Josephology.

To paraphrase Augustine: to know God is to know myself, and to know myself is to know God. (cf. Augustine, De Trinitate X.9; Confessions V.4.7)

Chapter One: Beginning at the Cross

In this chapter, I will explore Joseph’s backstory, his kinship network, his work, and his hometown—because understanding Joseph is essential for understanding the early life of Jesus and the formation of the Church.

I want to begin in a place that may seem unexpected: the Cross, where four women stood in fidelity: Mary the Mother of Jesus, Mary of Clopas, Mary Salome, and Mary Magdalene. Joseph was not present, yet these women help reveal his world, his relationships, and the clan network that shaped Jesus’ early life.

Mary of Clopas: The First Window Into Joseph’s World

Early Christian tradition holds that Clopas was Joseph’s blood brother, making Mary of Clopas Joseph’s sister‑in‑law. Clopas himself appears in the Gospel narrative as the disciple on the Road to Emmaus on Easter Sunday, one of the first witnesses to the risen Christ. His wife, Mary, was among the few holy women who remained faithful to Jesus even unto the Cross.

Mary of Clopas was the mother of James, Jude, Simeon, and Joses. James and Jude were two of the Twelve Apostles, and Simeon became the second bishop of Jerusalem. Scripture refers to them as Jesus’ “brothers,” but the biblical use of brother and sister is broader than modern usage. These terms often describe extended family; kinspersons within a clan network.

This clan network is the key to unlocking Joseph’s life, work, and influence.

Joseph as the Hidden Foundation

When we trace these relationships, we begin to see Joseph not merely as a quiet figure in the background, but as the hidden human foundation upon which Jesus built His early community. This does not compete with Christ being the Rock and Foundation of the Church. Rather, it shows how Jesus entered into a real, structured kinship world; one that Joseph stewarded.

It is significant that Jesus’ earliest apostles and leaders were introduced to Him through Joseph’s clan network. Joseph’s life was not incidental; it was formative.

In the next post, we will move to another kinswoman at the Cross, Mary Salome, and begin exploring Joseph’s kinship structure, his work, and his hometown - all of which illuminate the hidden architecture of the Incarnation.

Mary Salome: Matriarch of Jesus’ Galilean Home

Following the order of relationship to Joseph, we now turn to Mary Salome, wife of Zebedee, who emerges as the key matriarch of Jesus’ Galilean ministry.

The first striking detail is John’s decision not to name her. He identifies her simply as Jesus’ mother’s sister. John does the same with himself, the beloved disciple. The traditional explanation is humility, but the textual effect is richer: it becomes a typology for each of us. Every disciple is invited into the role of the beloved. Every Christian becomes a child of Mary. And as children of the Father, we become—analogously and to a lesser degree—brothers and sisters of the Mother of God.

The second observation is more personal. If I place myself in John’s sandals, Jesus entrusts His mother to John in front of John’s own mother. The sorrow of the moment would have eclipsed everything else, but over time it is a reality one would have to process. Scripture tells us John obeyed immediately. It is reasonable to assume that Salome aligned herself with her son’s new vocation, recognizing the gravity of what Jesus asked of him.

Third, this moment marks a transfer of vocation. John receives the baton from Joseph: protector of Mary, guardian of the Mother of the Divine Child. John’s role will unfold in later posts, but the handoff itself is unmistakable. The Galilean household—Zebedee, Salome, James, and John—stands at the hinge point between Nazareth and the Passion.

In the next couple of posts, we will turn to Zebedee, his clan network, the “sons of thunder,” and their fishing associates, Peter and Andrew. Together they form the backbone of Jesus’ Galilean home, the community from which His public ministry first took shape.

Salome’s Presence at the Cross and the Logic of John’s Guardianship

It is fitting that Salome would assent to John taking responsibility for Mary. She already had an older son, James, which aligns with the biblical expectation that the care of a widow falls to the household’s next available son. The text is silent on Zebedee’s lifespan, and pressing that silence too far becomes conjecture. What matters is that John’s guardianship of Mary is properly ordered within the kinship norms of Second‑Temple Judaism.

Salome’s Bold Petition

The Gospels give us another glimpse of Salome in Matthew 20:20–23. She approaches Jesus and boldly asks that her two sons sit at His right and left in the Kingdom. Jesus rebukes the request, noting that she does not understand the suffering bound up in such positions, and that those seats are already appointed—traditionally interpreted as belonging to Mary and Joseph: the two who most perfectly shared His earthly mission. And who now participate uniquely in the order of the hypostatic union—crowned above the angels in their proximity to the Incarnate Son.

Textually, this moment reveals more than maternal ambition. It signals a deeper kinship than mere discipleship. Her confidence carries the tone of someone who knows Jesus from His youth, someone embedded in the same clan network, someone accustomed to family‑first obligations. A secondary layer may be the substantial financial and ministerial support her household provided to Jesus’s public ministry. But the primary signal is relational proximity.

The Clan Network Behind Jesus’s Ministry

Widening the aperture, we see how Jesus’s selection of Joseph was not only grounded in Joseph’s righteousness but also in the strength of his clan network. This network becomes visible in the composition of the apostolic circle itself. Nearly half of the apostles trace back to Zebedee’s household or its immediate relational orbit.

In the next post, we will examine Zebedee, his role within this network, and how his household became one of the structural pillars of Jesus’s ministry.

Mary Salome, Clan Networks, and the Hidden Architecture of the Kingdom

Before turning to Zebedee and the significance of his Galilean fishing enterprise, we need to close the loop on Mary Salome. John’s Gospel refers to her simply as “His mother’s sister” (John 19:25).

This raises an immediate historical question. Early Christian memory consistently holds that Mary, the Mother of Jesus, was the only child of Anne and Joachim, who were advanced in age when she was conceived. The textual witness reinforces this: Mary’s betrothal to Joseph occurs at the moment she ages out of Temple service, with no indication of siblings or extended parental support. She is a young woman with no surviving parents, no brothers or sisters, and no household to return to.

This absence of a natal home makes Joseph’s role even more decisive. When Mary leaves the Temple, she does not return to family—she enters Joseph’s guardianship. Her vulnerability highlights the strength of Joseph’s clan network.

Clan Networks Across Regions

This is where Zebedee enters the picture. Zebedee lived in Galilee, while Joseph lived in Nazareth. The only plausible connective tissue between these households is a shared clan system—a network of kinspersons extending across regions. Scripture itself gives us a parallel: Zechariah and Elizabeth, Mary’s kinspersons, lived in Judea, several days’ journey away. Clan identity, not geography, bound them together.

Zebedee’s Fishing Enterprise

The Gospels present Zebedee as the owner of a fishing business in Bethsaida, operating with his sons James and John, alongside Peter and Andrew. John’s Gospel adds another layer: Philip is also from Bethsaida, and he immediately seeks out Nathanael after encountering Jesus.

This cluster of relationships—Peter, Andrew, James, John, Philip, Nathanael—cannot be explained merely by business ties or casual acquaintance. The density of association suggests a deeper social fabric: shared clan identity, intermarriage, and longstanding trust.

Salome’s Role and the Apostolic Network

Whatever the precise genealogical connection, the textual pattern is unmistakable: half of the apostolic circle emerges through Zebedee and his wife, Salome. Their household is one of the principal relational hubs around Jesus.

This illuminates Joseph’s hidden work. For thirty years, Joseph safeguarded the mystery of the Incarnation, the Nativity, and the Sonship of Jesus (Luke 2:49). But he was not only forming the Messiah—he was quietly forming the Kingdom of God. The clan networks Joseph maintained and strengthened became the relational scaffolding upon which Jesus would later call His apostles.

Joseph is the hidden craftsman of the Church, shaping the human architecture that would carry the Gospel into the world.

Mary Magdalene: The Maternal Witness

Moving to the next woman at the Cross—ordered here by her relational proximity to Joseph—we come to Mary Magdalene. She is the only woman named at Calvary who is not explicitly tied to Joseph’s kinship network. Yet her steadfast presence at the Cross deserves careful reflection, for even though she stands slightly outside the strict boundaries of Josephology, her role in Jesus’ life reveals a profoundly Josephine character.

We first meet Mary Magdalene in Luke’s Gospel, among the earliest women disciples: “Mary, called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out, and Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod’s steward, and Susanna, and many others, who provided for them out of their means.” (Luke 8:2–3). From this single sentence, the Evangelist gives us a surprising amount of clarity about her identity as a first‑century Jewish woman.

Her grouping with Joanna—who moves within Herod’s inner administrative circle—implies that Mary Magdalene herself belonged to a socially prominent class. Luke names her first, signaling either her spiritual mission as the apostle to the apostles or her elevated status within the community. We also learn that she was a woman of means. Her ability to travel freely with Jesus and the disciples indicates financial independence, personal agency, and wealth sufficient to support the ministry.

At the Cross, she stands among the matriarchs of Jesus’ kinship. When this is considered alongside her mobility, resources, and leadership among the women, a picture emerges of a mature, maternal figure—likely of similar age and stature to the other Marys. Her maternal role is further confirmed by her participation in the burial rites: going to the tomb at dawn to anoint the Lord’s body, a deeply maternal act in first‑century Judaism.

The tradition of her maternal love is richly attested by the saints: Gregory praises her for loving 'more'; Ambrose for tending the Lord’s body; Augustine for a love that 'burned'; Bernard for maternal tears; Bede for 'maternal diligence'; and Aquinas for perfected charity. (Gregory, Hom. Ev. 25.1; Ambrose, De Virg. 3.14.78; Augustine, Tract. in Io. 121.3; Bernard, Serm. Cant. 33.4; Bede, Hom. Ev. II.7; Aquinas, Catena Aurea, John 20:11)

“How tender are the tears of Magdalene! They are the tears of one who loves as a mother loves — with a heart that cannot abandon Him.”
— Bernard of Clairvaux, Sermones in Cantica 33.4

There is no explicit genealogical link between Mary Magdalene and Joseph. Yet her presence at the Cross, her provision for Jesus during His ministry, and her maternal devotion in His Passion reveal a Joseph-like vocation: she cared for Jesus in the absence of Joseph. Her love was so steadfast that she alone remained at the tomb, weeping. Because she stayed, she became the first individual witness of the Resurrection — the Apostle to the Apostles.

For the purposes of this book, I adhere to the Western Christian tradition of the past sixteen centuries, identifying Mary Magdalene with the sister of Martha and Lazarus. This choice is not made to argue historical precision but to serve the theological and narrative aims of Josephology. Whether she is one woman or two, the story unfolds the same. Yet the unified figure provides a poetic bridge between Galilee and Judea, strengthening the relational architecture of Jesus’ chosen homes.

The conflated view provides beautiful symmetry of the four Marys at the Cross representing Jesus’ three earthly refuges:

  • Mother Mary and Mary of Clopas — the hidden years in Nazareth,
  • Mary Salome — the Galilean ministry,
  • Mary Magdalene — Bethany, His sanctuary near Jerusalem in the days leading to His Passion.

Saint John The Apostle

The last person to reflect on who stood at the Cross is John the Apostle, son of Zebedee and Salome. In his Gospel, he consistently refers to himself—and even his family—in the third person. This narrative discipline serves a twofold purpose: humility and universality. By effacing himself, John allows every believer to step into the place of “the disciple” and receive the scene personally rather than biographically.

When Jesus saw his mother, and the disciple whom He loved standing near, He said to his mother, “Woman, behold, your son!” Then he said to the disciple, “Behold, your mother!” And from that hour the disciple took her to his own home. (John 19:26–27)

John as the New Joseph

In this moment, John becomes the new Joseph. He is entrusted with taking Mary into his home, echoing the angelic command to Joseph: “Do not be afraid to take Mary your wife.” Patristic writers consistently interpreted this as Joseph taking Mary into his household and assuming responsibility for her protection.

Yet the missions of Joseph and John diverge in their orientation. Joseph’s vocation was to conceal the mysteries of the Incarnation—shielding Mary and the Christ Child from danger, misunderstanding, and premature revelation. John’s vocation is the inverse: to reveal the mystery Joseph once hid. He opens his Gospel with the thunderous declaration of divinity—“In the beginning was the Word… and the Word was God.” If one were to summarize John’s Gospel in a single line, it would be: Jesus is God.

It is fitting, then, that the virginal apostle receives the mission of the virginal father. John becomes the guardian of the Mother of God, not by biological authority but by divine commission. In him, Joseph’s protective role is transposed into the apostolic age.

John as the New Benjamin

At the Cross, John also becomes the new Benjamin. In Genesis, Benjamin becomes the beloved son of Jacob who lost his wife Rachel shortly after childbirth and his son Joseph, presumed dead after being sold into slavery by his brothers. The pattern reappears in its perfected form at the Cross.

Jesus, the true New Joseph, is betrayed by His brothers, handed over, and dies. Mary, the new Rachel, suffers the sword that pierces her heart as she participates fully in the sacrifice of her Divine Son. And John, standing beside her, becomes the beloved son—the one entrusted to Mary after the death of the firstborn.

Jesus will rise and save the world, just as Joseph rose to save his brothers. But in the interim between death and resurrection, Mary receives a son, and John receives a mother. The typology is not merely literary; it is theological. The Cross becomes the moment where the Old Testament family is recast into its New Covenant fulfillment.

Our Lady of Sorrows

Mary's role at the Cross is not only maternal, it is also spousal. Joseph is spouse of Mary. Mary is spouse of the Holy Spirit. Joseph, therefore, is the spouse of the spouse of the Holy Spirit. As the New Eve, Mary is also spouse of the New Adam. Finally, as the bride of Christ she is the prototype of the Church, which was born on Calvary as blood and water poured from the side of Christ. As the sword pierced His Sacred Heart, her Immaculate Heart, so perfectly united to His Sacred Heart participated in this moment. These are the spiritual birth pangs prophesied in John's Book of Revelation.

As spouse of Mary, Joseph's Most Chaste heart also shares union with the Immaculate Heart of Mary and Sacred Heart of Jesus. He is rightly called the lowest member of the highest order – the Order of the Hypostatic Union. The the true and legal spouse of Mary at the time of the Angel Gabriel's Annunciation of the Incarnation, Joseph is a member of this order. The Hypostatic Union in which Jesus took on human flesh, becoming God-Man, fully human and fully divine – One Person; two natures.

Josephology Flows from Christology through Mariology

Mariology flows from Christology and returns back to Christ. Josephology flows from Christology and Mariology and returns back to the Hearts of Jesus and Mary. This theology of Holy Family becomes another icon of the Most Holy Trinity in which the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son (not to take anything away from the Holy Family as mirror of the Trinity: Jesus as Son, Mary as Spouse of the Holy Spirit, and Joseph as Father. This formula of Josephology flowing from Christology through Mariology, Joseph becomes an icon of the Holy Spirit. The hidden person protecting the Incarnation, never speaking of himself, and always pointing us to his Divine Son and Mother of God.

We also see in Joseph a fulfillment of the prophet Isaiah and the Seven Gifts of the Holy Spirit. While this passage is clearly referring to the Messiah Who is the Christ, the Anointed One; we can also see a typology of Joseph, the just man. The Church Fathers have consistently taught that the distinction as a just man (dikaios in Matthew 19) was a full possession the Gifts of the Holy Spirit.

There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots. And the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord. And his delight shall be in the fear of the Lord. (Isaiah 11:1-2)

Joseph of Arimathea

Although not explicitly at the Cross, honorable mention goes to Joseph of Arimathea.

Saint Luke describes him as a good and righteous man. Thus, sharing with Saint Joseph name and character - just man. He is a type of Joseph and worthy of mention.

What I find significant about this Joseph is that he protected the Body of Christ after His death. He boldly went to Pilate and asked for the Body. He then took the body, wrapped it in a linen cloth, and placed it in a tomb where nobody had been laid – virgin womb to virgin tomb.

This linen cloth has not only been preserved to this day, it has become one of the most studied artifacts in history. Its blood stains capture the brutality of the Crucifixion down to minute details. The image was most likely generated by a blast of pure light energy, absent of any heat - capturing the moment of the Resurrection. This Joseph's act of heroic virtue protected the Body of Christ from Cross to Tomb and continues to protect it through the miraculous Shroud of Turin.

Geography of Joseph's Life

Now that we have met the Holy Women and the Apostle at the Cross, we can turn to their geography and history. Through the four Marys at the Cross, we are introduced to the major geographic regions of first‑century Israel — Judea, Galilee, and Samaria.

These three areas represent the historical memory of the twelve tribes of Israel, the framework upon which Jesus built His Church through the twelve apostles. Judea is the remnant of Judah, the tribe that survived the various captivities and exiles. Galilee represents the tribes of the former Northern Kingdom, which broke away during the reign of King Rehoboam, the son of Solomon. The Southern Kingdom became Judah and retained the kingship in Jerusalem, even though it later fell under foreign powers — Persians, Greeks, and Romans.

Samaria

Although not directly represented at the Cross, Samaria is a remnant of two of the twelve tribes and deserves mention. Joseph of Old received a double blessing from his father Jacob, and thus Scripture speaks of the tribes of Ephraim and Manasseh rather than a tribe of Joseph. These two tribes eventually separated from the others and established a rival temple in Samaria. Again, we can see Joseph of Old hidden behind his children as a type of Joseph being hidden behind his Divine Son.

This background sheds light on the encounter with the Samaritan woman at the well. Likewise, the counting of the twelve tribes — in which Levi is not counted and Joseph is counted twice — illuminates the mystery of the Upper Room at Pentecost. While it is tempting to explore these ideas further, it is beyond the scope of this work. My hope is that this work provides a framework upon which further theological study and devotional reflection may be built.

Judea

The Cross of Calvary stood just outside Jerusalem and only a few miles from Bethany. Bethany was Jesus’ chosen place of refuge during the week before His Passion and death. It was here that Mary anointed His feet with oil and where He raised Lazarus from the dead.

Jerusalem was the center of worship and kingship. Volumes could be written about this city. Seeing it through the lens of Josephology sheds additional light on the life of Christ. For example, Joseph was the non‑royal father of the King, whereas Jerusalem had fallen under rulers who did not possess true sonship. More on this will be explored in the later chapter on Jesse.

Another aspect of Judea worth noting is the tribe of Benjamin, which aligned itself with Judah and was eventually absorbed into it. This is fitting in light of the story in Genesis where Judah offers himself in place of Benjamin — the moment that signaled to Joseph that his brothers had repented and allowed him to reveal himself. This is yet another typology of Joseph hiding the mystery of the Incarnation and Jesus revealing Himself in the fullness of time.

Moving from the Cross to the Nativity, Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea, then exiled to Egypt, and raised in Nazareth. Bethlehem — “house of bread” — was essential in establishing Jesus’ Davidic lineage. Again, this backdrop gives us another angle from which to reflect on the Nativity in light of Josephology.

Galilee

Nazareth was an agricultural village of roughly twenty to forty family units, each consisting of five to ten members, for a total population of about 150–200 people. Its primary production included olives, grapes (wine), wheat, and barley. A carpenter — more broadly, a tekton — in a town of this size would be known by all and called upon to build houses, repair roofs, and fashion harnesses for livestock. Again, we can see how Joseph’s work formed the Messiah, who used images of vineyards, olive trees, and yokes in His teaching. Even stories such as the one in which a man is lowered through a roof remind us of Joseph.

Nazareth was centrally located in Israel: four miles south of Sepphoris (a 60–90 minute walk); about seventy miles north of Jerusalem, Bethany, and Bethlehem (a three‑day caravan); fifty‑five miles north of Samaria; twenty miles south of the Sea of Galilee (Capernaum, Bethsaida, and Magdala); and nine miles south of Cana. Faithful Jews traveled to Jerusalem three times per year — Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles. With six days of round‑trip travel plus week‑long festivities, these pilgrimages lasted about two weeks.

Nazareth was just far enough off the main road to keep the mystery of the Incarnation hidden until the appointed time. It sat just beyond the caravan route between Capernaum and Jerusalem. It was likely one such caravan that Mary joined to visit Elizabeth, and another in which Jesus became hidden from His parents for nearly a day.

These caravans give us a glimpse into daily life. The notion of single‑family residences and nuclear families was unheard of. Families lived in clans — multi‑generational households including in‑laws, siblings, cousins, aunts, and uncles. There was no strict distinction between blood brothers and cousins as there is today.

These clans remained connected even when relocating. We see this vividly when Mary visits Elizabeth. We also see it in Joseph’s relationships with Mary of Clopas and Mary Salome. It is also the most plausible connection between Joseph and Zebedee, who likely remained in contact during pilgrimages and through Sepphoris.

There is a non‑biblical town of historical significance that leads us to Capernaum. Sepphoris was home to the Sanhedrin and a center of religious, cultural, and political life. It was ethnically diverse, shaped by foreign influence and large building projects — synagogues, Roman temples, bathhouses, public buildings, theaters, and statues. It is very likely that Joseph commuted to Sepphoris during peak construction seasons. The commute was about an hour. Jesus likely joined Joseph as He came of age. For nearly twenty years, Joseph and Jesus likely spent hours walking to and from job sites. It is interesting that historically Nazareth is tiny village in the shadow of Sepphoris but Bibically Nazareth is home of the Messiah and Sepphoris in not mentioned.

This brings us to Capernaum, a fishing town on the Sea of Galilee. Its distance from Jerusalem, its population, and its proximity to larger cities made it an ideal place for Jesus to center His earthly ministry and build His Kingdom. Furthermore, the theological significance of reuniting the lost tribes of Israel is profound.

Seven Sorrows & Joys of Saint Joseph

In this chapter, I will walk through the Biblical narratives of Joseph, using the devotion of the Seven Sorrow & Joys of Saint Joseph. This devotion developed slowly over the centuries. It a pre-existing, tried and true, structure, as a framework to dive into the Biblical narrative of Saint Joseph. We've looked at how Joseph's family clan was chosen for the formation of the Messiah. We've looked at where he raised Jesus. Next we look who is Saint Joseph, just man, loving husband, protective father, devoted worker, and Son of David.

Joseph received his own annunciation regarding the Incarnation. Matthew records the moment,

Now the birth of Jesus Christ took place in this way. When his mother Mary had been betrothed to Joseph, before they came together she was found to be with child of the Holy Spirit; and her husband Joseph, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame, resolved to send her away quietly. But as he considered this, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to him in a dream, saying, “Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife, for that which is conceived in her is of the Holy Spirit; she will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.”

Looking at each phrase of this passage, the first reflection is Mary and Joseph's betrothal. Matthew is clear that Joseph was the husband of Mary at the time of the Incarnation. The Gospel of Luke states that Mary was espoused – or betrothed – to Joseph when the Angel Gabriel appears to her. The Gospel of Matthew says the same at the time of the Annunciation to Joseph. Matthew goes on to state that Joseph was husband of Mary. Joseph was the husband of Mary and, therefore, true and legal father of Jesus at the moment of the Incarnation – when He was conceived in Mary by the power of the Holy Spirit.

Next is the phrase "before they came together" simply means before Joseph took her into his home. This anchors the Incarnation and Nativity of Jesus on Mary's Perpetual Virginity. This is critical to understanding Christology. Mary was a virgin before, during, and after the birth of Jesus. The Gospels are establishing that Jesus was the prophesied Messiah, conceived by the Holy Spirit, and born of a Virgin. The Blessed Virgin Mary gave birth to a Person, a Divine Person, the Second Person of the Most Holy Trinity – she is the Mother of God.

Just Man

Immediately before Joseph's resolution to quietly divorce Mary, Saint Matthew describes Joseph as a "just man". This is in perfect complementarity to Mary being "full of grace". In fact, one can assert that Joseph's righteousness flows from the storehouse of grace that has been entrusted to Mary, Mediatrix of All Grace.

Church Tradition teaches us that everyone is given the grace needed for the mission. As spouse of Mary at the moment of the Incarnation, Joseph is a member of the Hypostatic Union. His place in heaven, therefore, is higher than the angels, higher than the apostles and martyrs. He sits at Jesus' left with Mary as His right – the right Queen of Heaven and the left Prince Over All His Possessions (cf. Proverbs 3.16, Genesis 41.40, 45.8). His mission is the formation and protection of the Messiah is the greatest mission given to a man. Joseph was given grace for the mission – a superabundance of grace second only to his spouse, Mary, plentitude or full of grace. As Prince, Joseph's mission is protection, whereas Mary's is distribution of all the grace earned by infinite merits of the Divine Son.

Justice means giving God and man what is rightly due to them. God deserves our adoration and worship. Man deserves dignity and love as a human person crested in the image of God. Justice means being in right relationship with God – completely docile, obedient, and aligned with God's Will. Joseph was the closest man to Christ, closest to God.

Justice is also one of the four cardinal virtues, among the seven human virtues - faith, hope, charity; Justice, prudence, temperance, and fortitude. Joseph was given the grace to fully live out theses virtues.

Lastly, Joseph had full possession of the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit - Fear of God, wisdom; piety, counsel; knowledge, understanding; and fortitude. These gifts fully blossomed into the fruits of the Spirit – love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, gentleness, and self-control.

In Greek, justice and righteousness are expressed with the same word – dikaios. In Latin and the liturgy, justice is unfolded into two distinct words – right and just; dignum et iustus. For this Josephology project, Joseph is a just man (dikaios / iustus) and his actions are righteous (dikaios / dignum). The life of Saint Joseph was the embodiment of Eucharist worship - liturgy. He worshipped the Christ Child present in a baby, an adolescent, and young man, as no other man could.

At Mass, our highest form of worship, we say, "It is right and just, our duty and our salvation". From a liturgical context, justice is our response to God – adoration and worship – and liturgy is the fitting way to humans to worship God.

One last note on Divine Justice, the Incarnation, and the Order of the Hypostatic Union:

To satisfy divine justice, the Redeemer had to be both true God and true man. As man, Christ could truly suffer and die; as God, His suffering possessed infinite worth, sufficient to atone for the sins of the whole human race. This is the dogmatic principle articulated in the tradition and summarized in the Catechism of Pope Pius X: the Incarnation is necessary for salvation, because only a divine Person assuming human nature could offer a sacrifice of infinite value.

Mary’s fiat was necessary in a subordinate and instrumental sense. By her free consent, the Word took flesh; thus her cooperation is truly part of the divine economy, though entirely dependent on God’s initiative and ordered to Christ’s redemptive mission.

Joseph’s fiat was not necessary for the objective redemption, but it was necessary for the providential protection of the Incarnate Word. His obedience safeguarded the life of Jesus from conception through adulthood, ensuring that the saving work of the Cross could be historically accomplished. Joseph’s mission is therefore not constitutive of salvation, but indispensable within God’s providential plan for the concrete unfolding of the Incarnation in history.

Found to be with Child of the Holy Spirit

Next is the phrase "found to be with Child of the Holy Spirit." Without diving into Greek, the text implies that Joseph discovered her pregnancy visibly. The most plausible explanation is that when she returned from the birth of John the Baptist, Joseph could see that she was pregnant. He most likely knew that Mary visited Elizabeth to serve her final three months of pregnancy.
I want to pause for a moment and reflect on the Annunciation to Mary and her discernment. She asked, "how can this be." Part of the angel's response was behold your kinswoman Elizabeth is expecting. This, I believe, was part of the discernment process and evidence of divine origin.

Likewise, it was part of the discernment process for Saint Joseph. He almost certainly had heard from Mary and kinspeople of the "the wonders surrounding Elizabeth’s conception." As far as Elizabeth's greeting and Mary's Magnificat, there is no textual evidence that they shared these divine mysteries with other. It was God's message, not theirs, to deliver - so they pondered them in their hearts, not voiced out loud.

When Joseph saw that Mary was with Child, his knowledge of Elizabeth was evidence of divine origin. Furthermore, as a just man and son of David, Joseph was most likely extremely knowledgeable of the prophesies of the Messiah, "the Virgin shall conceive" (Isaiah 7:14). He would have been intimately familiar with the miracle births of the Patriarchs – Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph.

Furthermore, Joseph loves Mary and knows of her holiness and vow of virginity. It is not plausible that the just man would doubt his wife, or be suspicious of adultery – it had to be of divine origin.

Lastly, we cannot rule out that Joseph received infused knowledge or divine peace.

Unwilling to Put to Shame; Divorce Quietly

Saint Matthew gives us only option that Saint Joseph considered – divorce her quietly so as to protect her from shame. Mosaic divorce law allowed "a man to write a certificate of divorce, and to put her away … for hardness of heart." (Mark 10:5, cf. Deut. 24:1-4) She would then be free to remarry but he could never again take her as his wife. This had to have been an agonizing decision but resolved to send her away quietly. Joseph chose the path that honors both justice and mercy.

Joseph did not feel worthy to take her into his home knowing her holiness and the child was of God and trust in God's protection. He is choosing the option like so many patriarchs and saints before him:

  • Moses: "Who I am I that I should go to Pharoah?" (Exodus 3-4)
  • Abraham: "I am but dust and ashes." (Genesis 18:27)
  • Jeremiah: “I do not know how to speak; I am only a youth.” (Jeremiah 1:6)
  • David: "Who am I, O Lord GOD, and what is my house, that You have brought me this far?” (2 Samuel 7:18)
  • Elizabeth: “Who am I, that the mother of my Lord should come to me?” (Luke 1:43)
  • John the Baptist: “I am not worthy to untie the strap of his sandal.” (John 1:27; Matthew 3:14)- Peter: “Depart from me, Lord, for I am a sinful man.” (Luke 5:8)
  • The Centurion “Lord, I am not worthy that you should enter under my roof.” (Matthew 8:8; Luke 7:6–7)

The Annunciation to Joseph

Just as an angel intervened before Abraham could sacrifice Isaac, an angel intervened before Joseph could divorce Mary:

Joseph, son of David, do not fear to take Mary your wife, for that which is conceived in her is of the Holy Spirit; she will bear a son, and you shall call his name Jesus, for he will save his people from their sins.

Joseph's fear was fear of the Lord – a holy reverence of God not wishing to do anything against God's will. Joseph's decision was Wisdom – being of one will in union with God. Joseph's discernment was knowledge and understanding, piety and counsel – Joseph not only knew the law, he understood it; his daily piety gave him the counsel needed to make the right choice in any given situation. Joseph's decision was made with fortitude, knowing the gravity of the mission before him.

The angel was the witness, a messenger directly from God, that Joseph needed to take Mary into his home without any fear of violating the law or fear of displeasing God. He did not hesitate, "When Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him." (Matthew 1:24)

The mystery still to be contemplated, not necessarily to understand, is that Mary is spouse of the Holy Spirit through the Incarnation and the wife of Joseph. Both spousal unions are virginal pointing us to heaven and our union with Christ. "God became man so that man could become God."

Lastly, to the outside world, while socially unusual, Joseph’s decision was perfectly legal. There was no law against a husband having relations with his wife at any stage of marriage. Except for those few to whom God revealed the mystery of the Incarnation – Mary, Joseph, and Elizabeth – Jesus was Joseph's son, the carpenter's son (Matthew 13:55, Mark 6:3, Luke 4:22, John 6:24). It was a secret that Joseph carried to the grave. God provided a way out for Joseph to protect the Mother, the Child, and the mystery itself until the fullness of time.

The Holy Women at the Cross, and Joseph's kinship network, bear witness that Joseph's decision was accepted and respected by his clan network. Whether those outside his inner circle saw him with suspicion is likely. He was a suffering servant not because of being ostracized but by union with Sacred Heart of Jesus and Immaculate Heart of Mary.

Discernment

Joseph's story of discernment parallels the Church's traditional discernment Ignatian discernment process:

  • First Consideration – gather the facts, analyze the details, he found her with Child by the Holy Spirit
  • Second Consideration - prayerfully weighing the interior movements, being a just man and unwilling to put her to shame
  • First Resolution – make a decision and live with the decision for a period of time, he resolved to divorce her quietly
  • Confirmation – stay open and docile to God's will, listen for peace and clarity about the decision, then an angel of the Lord appeared to him in his sleep.

This is the backbone of Catholic discernment and parallels Saint Francis de Sales and Saint Alphonsus Ligouri. However, Carmelite spirituality offers another discernment process that parallels Saint Joseph. It is more fluid and contemplative than step-by-step and analytical. Joseph's silence is the image of the Contemplative, listening for that still small whisper of God.

And he said, “Go forth, and stand upon the mount before the Lord.” And behold, the Lord passed by, and a great and strong wind rent the mountains, and broke in pieces the rocks before the Lord, but the Lord was not in the wind; and after the wind an earthquake, but the Lord was not in the earthquake; and after the earthquake a fire, but the Lord was not in the fire; and after the fire a still small voice. (1 Kings 19:11-12)

Climbing a mountain is the image of prayer – lifting one's heart and mind to God. The strong wind is the image of a situation that presents itself, that knocks us off balance. The earthquake questions whether the foundation we are standing on is solid ground. The fire is a symbol of purification. This discernment process is driven by prayer and purity. The still small whisper of God is our ability to be still and know that He is God, even in the midst of turmoil. It is a process of refinement and spiritual growth. If one is walking with God and after His own heart, one will exercise prudent judgement.

Joseph Son of David

The annunciation to Joseph parallels the annunciation to Mary with beautiful complementarity. Both annunciations share an angelic delivery of the message of the Incarnation to each. Mary is awake and receptive to the angel's greeting, whereas Joseph is asleep and ready for the responsibility. This detail is theologically significant and teaches the mission of each.

Mary's mission is maternal and interior. Her Annunciation, therefore, is conscious and awake. She hears the message, she asks how can this be, and she assents to the message (fiat). She is to be the Mother of God and bear the Incarnate Word in her womb. She will give birth to a Son in Bethlehem and give birth to the Church at Calvary.

Joseph's mission is paternal and exterior. His Annunciation, therefore, is direction and guidance. His is a a mission given in a dream. He awakes and obeys everything the angel commanded. He will protect the Child, His Mother, and the mystery of His Incarnation. The Messiah will grow in wisdom and in stature under Joseph's formation.

Another complementary parallel, Mary is addressed with the title "Full of Grace" (kecharitōmenē) whereas Joseph is addressed as "Son of David". Again this is the same title that Matthew gives to Jesus – like father, like son. Joseph is the new Adam, subordinate to Jesus as the new Adam. Joseph is the foundation of the Church, the stump of Jesse, subordinate to the Rock of Christ. Joseph is not competing with titles, he is sharing titles in a very unique way. Joseph is the foretaste of sharing in the Divine Life of the Most Holy Trinity - showing us the way.

Mary will bear a son, thus giving Jesus his human nature. Joseph shall name him Jesus, thus giving Jesus His human place. By this command, the angel is commissioning Joseph to be the foster father of the Jesus. Joseph is not the father by generation but by legal lineage and legal marriage – the true and legal father of Jesus. By naming Jesus, Joseph is taking on authority, responsibility, and protection of Jesus. No wonder he did not feel worthy to take Mary and Child under His roof.

"And He will save His people from their sins." Only Jesus could satisfy Divine Justice and Divine Mercy. Mary and Joseph mirror their Divine Son – Mary, the new Eve, mother of mercy and Joseph, the new patriarch, father of justice. Immediately after Joseph receive his mission, he learns of the mission of his Divine Son, to save people from sins. Modernism has pushed sin and hell to the realm of mythology. Josephology reminds us that hell is real, the wages of sin is death, and Jesus suffered and died for our sins.

Joseph Awoke and Obeyed

All this took place to fulfil what the Lord had spoken by the prophet: “Behold, a virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and his name shall be called Emman′u-el” (which means, God with us). When Joseph woke from sleep, he did as the angel of the Lord commanded him; he took his wife, but knew her not until she had borne a son;[l] and he called his name Jesus.

Joseph was truly asleep when the angel appeared. He was not in deep prayer, or ecstasy. He had made his decision, he was at peace with his decision, and his conscience was clear. Amidst the most perplexing of situations, he slept. Much like his Divine Son who slept on a boat in the middle of a severe storm.

Nativity of Jesus

Saint Matthew provides a one-sentence summary of the Nativity of Jesus, "In the time of King Herod, after Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea." We, therefore, will turn to Luke to get the details of the Nativity.

In those days a decree went out ... that all the world should be enrolled. ... And all went to be enrolled, each to his own city. And Joseph also went up from Galilee, from the city of Nazareth, to Judea, to the city of David, which is called Bethlehem, because he was of the house and lineage of David, to be enrolled with Mary, his betrothed, who was with child. And while they were there, the time came for her to be delivered. And she gave birth to her first-born son and wrapped him in swaddling cloths, and laid him in a manger... (Luke 2:1-7)

Luke's narrative of the birth of Jesus is patient and methodical. He paints a picture of trip that is planned and organized - a picture that is fitting of a just man, a protective husband and father.

Luke begins with the Caesar calling for a census. Our modern census is received in the mail and completed in a single sitting. On the other hand, today's is a much longer process - an act of congress, recruiting and training of volunteers, publicity, marketing, and distribution. In first century, however, people had to travel to their hometowns to be enrolled - a process that took days, not hours.

The distance from Nazareth to Bethlehem was 90 miles and about four days travel, plus the days needed for the census itself. A trip for a census would have been very similar to the pilgrimages to Jerusalem lasting a couple of weeks. Mary and Joseph would have traveled with kinspeople in a caravan. A caravan provided safety, food, and care. There would have been schedule rest stops and places to stay along the way. Since the pilgrimages occurred three times a year, this network was firmly established.

Furthermore, Jewish hospitality was not merely an act of kindness, it was covenantal. It was tangential to law and type of New Covenant liturgy. We read in the letter to the Hebrews, "Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares."

The hospitality of Abraham and three visitors elevated hospitality to covenantal. Before diving this story, recorded in Genesis 18, we need to get the context from the previous chapter 17:

When Abram was ninety-nine years old the Lord appeared to Abram, and said to him, “I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless. And I will make my covenant between me and you, and will multiply you exceedingly.” Then Abram fell on his face; and God said to him, “Behold, my covenant is with you, and you shall be the father of a multitude of nations. No longer shall your name be Abram, but your name shall be Abraham; for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations. I will make you exceedingly fruitful; and I will make nations of you, and kings shall come forth from you.

This is the moment when God established His covenant with Abraham. The name change from Abram, which means "exalted father" to Abraham which means "father of nations" was an increase. It was a typology of Joseph, whose name means "he will increase". The covenant with Abraham increased the People of God from marriage (Adam & Eve) to family (Noah) to tribal kinship (Abraham). The covenant will later be renewed with Moses (nation), David (Kingdom), and lastly Jesus establishing a New Covenant expanding the People of God to all peoples through His One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church. Joseph is the last and greatest patriarch of the Old Covenant and the first father of the New Covenant. He stands at the hinge of history being the man chosen for formation of the Messiah:

And he went down with them and came to Nazareth, and was obedient to them; and his mother kept all these things in her heart. And Jesus increased in wisdom and in stature, and in favor with God and man. (Luke 2:51-52)

That brings us to Chapter 18 in which Abraham is visited by three strangers and promised a son, even though he and Sarah were 100 years old. The three visitors is a typology of the Most Holy Trinity. Abraham addresses them as Lord and bows before them – a gesture only fitting for God. Abraham asks them to stay, he provides them with water, bread, milk, and calf. They sat by a tree and ate. They asked for Sarah and gave her the promise of a son. This moment expanded Jewish hospitality laws (Exodus 23:9) into covenantal responsibility. This would be the foundation for the structured hospitality that we see in Mary and Joseph at the Nativity, finding the Boy Jesus in the Temple, and Jesus' travel ministry, especially that of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus in Bethany.

This hospitality is also echoed in the story of the Road to Emmaus. Clopas and his companion asked a stranger to stay with them. In the breaking of the bread, Jesus revealed himself to them. This story of hospitality was the primordial structure of the liturgy that still exists today – gathering rite, penitential rite, Liturgy of the Word, Liturgy of the Eucharist, and dismissal.

Saint Joseph is the hidden in each of these stories - Abraham's increase, brother of Clopas, and the hospitality of Joseph's clan network.

Glory to God in the Highest

And the angel said to them, “Be not afraid; for behold, I bring you good news of a great joy which will come to all the people; for to you is born this day in the city of David a Savior, who is Christ the Lord. And this will be a sign for you: you will find a babe wrapped in swaddling cloths and lying in a manger.” And suddenly there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly host praising God and saying,
“Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace among men with whom he is pleased!”
When the angels went away from them into heaven, the shepherds said to one another, “Let us go over to Bethlehem and see this thing that has happened, which the Lord has made known to us.” And they went with haste, and found Mary and Joseph, and the babe lying in a manger. And when they saw it they made known the saying which had been told them concerning this child; and all who heard it wondered at what the shepherds told them. But Mary kept all these things, pondering them in her heart. And the shepherds returned, glorifying and praising God for all they had heard and seen, as it had been told them. (Luke 2:14-20)

Joseph and Mary were in the stable room at the Judean domestic house when the angels appeared to the shepherds, who were out in the field. Other than the supernatural, the only way that we have an account of this story is from the shepherds, which is supported by the text. The shepherds found the baby lying in a manger and share the story of the angels.

The key takeaway to point out is that "all who heard it wondered" and, separately, "Mary pondered". If the shepherds appeared only to Mary and Joseph, there would be no need for the two separate accounts of wondering and pondering. The text, therefore, supports the notion of a Judaean domestic house with a main quarters for the family, guest quarters (kataluma), and stable quarters. Furthermore, the phrase "all who heard it" infers that there were several others present, likely the host family and at least one other family who were witnesses to this astonishing story of the shepherds.

The text does not specifically state that Mary and Joseph chose the stable, but the Church Fathers consistently teach that the significance of this detail is the humility of Mary, Joseph, and Jesus. Humility is the only virtue that Jesus practices because every other virtue is by nature of being God. Jesus is God, and by nature, True, Good, and Beautiful. He cannot grow in virtue but He chose humility – He chose to become man, to condescend to us to save us. This is why it is the only virtue that Jesus said, "learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart." (Matt 11:29)

Lastly, it seems only fitting that Jesus would choose shepherds for the Gloria – the heavenly worship of the Birth of the Messiah - because of the beautiful symmetry with Saint Joseph. The shepherds received the message in the dark of night – Joseph at night in his sleep. The shepherds were responsible for protect the flock – Joseph protected the the Holy Family. The shepherds went in haste to find the babe – Joseph awoke immediately and obeyed. The shepherds and the carpenter – humble workers and guardians of the flock. This Josephology work is a product of the Church recognizing Saint Joseph's continued protection of the flock – the Church – in 1870 as Patron of the Universal Church.
